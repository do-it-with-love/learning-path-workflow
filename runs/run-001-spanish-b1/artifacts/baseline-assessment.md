# Baseline Assessment — European Spanish

## Summary
The learner is placed at **pre-A1** on the CEFR six-level scale (Common European
Framework of Reference for Languages: A1–C2) — below the first defined proficiency
level, i.e. not yet meeting the Breakthrough (A1) descriptors for listening, speaking,
reading, or writing. This is a **light-mode assessment**: no diagnostic was designed or
administered. The placement is asserted directly from the learner's confirmed
self-report in `requirements.md` (approximately 200 words picked up casually, no
grammar, no prior study or classes) rather than from any test performance. The gap
between this baseline and the confirmed target — strong A2 / B1-threshold practical
conversational competence in European (Castilian) Spanish — spans essentially the
entire A1 level plus most of A2, which is the gap module 1 onward must close.

## Findings

### Level scale
The learner's target language, Spanish, is one of the languages the CEFR was built to
describe, and Spanish-specific certification (the Instituto Cervantes' DELE diplomas)
is issued directly against CEFR levels, confirming CEFR is the standard, real-world
proficiency framework for this subject rather than an arbitrary choice.

CEFR defines six reference levels in three broad divisions:

| Division | Levels | What it broadly means |
|---|---|---|
| Basic User | A1 (Breakthrough), A2 (Waystage) | A1: can understand and use very basic, familiar everyday expressions and phrases aimed at concrete needs; can introduce self and others, ask/answer simple personal questions, interact only if the other person speaks slowly and helps. A2: can understand frequently-used expressions in areas of immediate relevance (basic personal/family info, shopping, local geography, employment); can communicate in simple, routine tasks requiring direct exchange of information on familiar matters. |
| Independent User | B1 (Threshold), B2 (Vantage) | B1: can deal with most situations likely to arise while travelling, produce simple connected text on familiar topics, describe experiences and plans, give reasons and explanations briefly. B2: can interact with fluency and spontaneity with native speakers, understand main ideas of complex text on concrete/abstract topics. |
| Proficient User | C1 (Advanced), C2 (Mastery) | Fluent, flexible, effective use of language for social, academic, and professional purposes; near-native comprehension and production. |

CEFR describes competence across four language activities — reception (listening,
reading), production (spoken, written), interaction, and mediation — at each level, not
just vocabulary count. This matters for placement: raw word count alone does not put a
learner "in" a level; what they can *do* with the language does.

CEFR itself does not define a formal level below A1. Where a learner's ability falls
short of every A1 descriptor, the standard convention (used informally across language
schools and by CEFR-aligned bodies) is to call this **pre-A1**. This report uses that
term explicitly as a convention, not as an official CEFR designation — see Open
Questions.

### Prerequisite gaps
Everything module 1 will need to assume as a starting point and that the learner
currently lacks:

1. **Spanish alphabet and sound-to-letter correspondences**, including letters/sounds
   with no close English equivalent (ñ, rr, j).
2. **Peninsular pronunciation norms specifically** — distinción (the /θ/–/s/ contrast
   in "c"/"z" vs "s", e.g. *cinco* vs *sí*), which does not exist in Latin American
   Spanish varieties the learner may have incidentally encountered in media; the learner
   has zero exposure to this distinction and it is not learnable by inference from
   casual vocabulary.
3. **Subject pronouns**, including the Peninsular-specific **vosotros** (2nd person
   plural familiar) and its associated verb endings — a form absent from Latin American
   Spanish and therefore not something casual, algorithm-driven media exposure would
   have supplied.
4. **Present-tense conjugation patterns** for regular -ar/-er/-ir verbs and the most
   common irregulars (ser, estar, tener, ir) — currently entirely absent.
5. **ser vs. estar** as a distinct grammatical concept — a foundational fork in Spanish
   grammar with no equivalent single-verb "to be" pattern in English, so it cannot be
   assumed as intuitive.
6. **Noun gender and number, and adjective agreement** — a structural feature of
   Spanish with no parallel in isolated English-cognate vocabulary.
7. **Basic sentence word order and question formation** (word order, question words
   qué/dónde/cuándo/cómo).
8. **Numbers, telling time, and basic survival phrases** as a taught, systematic set —
   not just whatever overlaps by chance with the ~200 casual words.

None of these can be assumed present. Module 1 must start at true beginner grammar and
pronunciation instruction, not at a "light refresher" pace, and must build in
Peninsular-specific pronunciation and vosotros exposure from the outset rather than as
a later add-on, since the learner's casual exposure — if it included any Spanish media
at all — more likely reflected Latin American norms (ustedes, no distinción) than
Peninsular ones, and that would need to be actively corrected, not just supplemented.

## Open Questions
- **"pre-A1" is not an official CEFR level.** The CEFR (per the Wikipedia article
  retrieved this run) defines six levels, A1 through C2, and does not itself specify a
  formal designation below A1. "Pre-A1" is used here as a widely-adopted pedagogical
  convention to describe a learner who does not yet meet the A1 descriptors, and it is
  flagged explicitly as such rather than presented as a standard CEFR level. If
  curriculum-architect or validator need a stricter framing, module 1 should simply be
  scoped as "CEFR A1, from zero" without relying on the pre-A1 label.
- **Exact content of the ~200-word vocabulary is unconfirmed.** `requirements.md` states
  a word count but not a word list. This report assumes (based on how casual,
  unstructured vocabulary exposure typically accumulates) that the set skews toward
  greetings, food/travel nouns, numbers, and English-Spanish cognates, and contains no
  functional grammar. This is a reasonable inference, not a confirmed inventory. If
  precision here matters before module 1 is built, the learner could be asked to write
  down the ~200 words they know; this was not done because light mode explicitly skips
  diagnostics.
- **Whether any of the learner's casual exposure was Peninsular or Latin American
  Spanish is unknown.** The prerequisite gaps above assume the conservative case (zero
  reliable Peninsular exposure) since requirements.md gives no source for the ~200
  words and most freely available casual Spanish media defaults to Latin American
  norms. If the learner's exposure happens to have been Peninsular already, the
  pronunciation gap is smaller than stated here, but treating it as present-until-proven
  is the safer default for module 1 planning.
