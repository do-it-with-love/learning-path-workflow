Conversational European Spanish for Travel — Your Learning Path

Overview

You are starting from casual exposure — about 200 words, no grammar — and want to be able to hold real conversations in European (Castilian) Spanish on a trip to Spain: ordering food, asking directions, checking into a place to stay, and talking with people you meet along the way. This path gets you there through video lessons anchored by daily speaking practice, with Peninsular pronunciation and the vosotros form built in from week one rather than added later.

One thing to know up front, because it shapes everything else: you originally asked for B1 in six months. A certified B1 typically takes 350–400 hours of study; at 5 hours a week for 26 weeks you have about 130. Rather than stretch the timeline or the weekly hours, you chose to keep both fixed and aim at a slightly different, still very useful target: a strong A2 / B1-threshold level of practical conversational competence — enough to travel, have real conversations, and read everyday signs and menus, without the full grammatical range (or the certificate) that "B1" formally implies. That was a deliberate trade-off, not a compromise you're stuck with, and it's the honest label used throughout this plan.

The videos here teach the grammar and vocabulary; the speaking practice built into every module is where the ability to actually use Spanish forms. Watching is necessary but not sufficient for your stated goal — treat the recorded exercises as the main event, not optional homework.

Before You Start

Prerequisites. None beyond what you already have. You're starting as a true beginner — no prior study assumed, and none needed.

What to have ready.

  • A phone or computer with a working microphone. Nearly every exercise in this plan asks you to record yourself and play it back — that's the core mechanism for catching your own errors, so a working mic is more important here than any app or subscription.
  • Somewhere to log new vocabulary as you go — a notebook or a simple spreadsheet is enough. This isn't strictly required until the final check, but starting it in week 1 will make that check far more meaningful than trying to reconstruct nine modules of vocabulary from memory at the end.

Your starting point. Because you're a confirmed absolute beginner, no diagnostic test was needed — your placement is asserted directly from what you reported: no functional grammar, and a small set of words picked up casually (most likely everyday nouns, numbers, greetings and cognates, not a taught or structured set). On the standard six-level scale used for language proficiency (A1 through C2), this places you below A1 — informally "pre-A1." Concretely, that means Module 1 assumes you know nothing about Spanish sounds, grammar, or sentence structure, including things you might expect to have picked up already: the alphabet and its sound rules, subject pronouns (especially vosotros, which doesn't exist in the Latin American Spanish that most casual media defaults to), verb conjugation, ser vs. estar, noun gender and agreement, and basic word order. If any of that already feels familiar, you'll move through Module 1 faster than planned — but nothing in this plan assumes it going in.

Modules

Module 1 — Sounds, Script & Survival Basics

Pre-A1 → A1.1 · Weeks 1–3

You'll learn to produce the Spanish sound system correctly — including the letters and sounds with no close English equivalent (ñ, rr, j) and, critically, the Peninsular distinción (the "th"-like sound for c/z before e/i, as in cinco, versus a plain "s"). You'll also learn the full set of subject pronouns including vosotros, count to 20, and handle a short greeting exchange — and learn to sound out unfamiliar written Spanish using letter-sound rules.

Watch and listen:

  • Absolute Beginners Spanish 4-10 — LightSpeed Spanish, alphabet, greetings and numbers, taught by a native Peninsular speaker.
  • Complete Spanish, Track 1 — Language Transfer (audio) — foundational sound and structure induction.
  • Mi Vida Loca, Episode 1 — BBC's Madrid-set beginner drama, survival basics and greetings.

Practice: Every session opens with a 10-minute rotating warm-up — odd sessions drill distinción word pairs by recording and comparing yourself (zapato/sapo, cielo/seis, perro/pero); even sessions recite pronouns, count to 20, and run a greeting exchange from memory. In week 2 you'll sound out an unfamiliar text aloud and record a 45–60 second self-introduction using at least one vosotros form. An optional speed-spelling stretch closes out week 3.

Checkpoint (20 min, end of week 3). Record four things back to back: the alphabet, ten words chosen to test ñ/rr/j/distinción (compared against a native clip), counting 0–20, and a 30-second greeting with a vosotros form. You're on track if you recite fluently without notes, at least 8 of 10 sounds match the native clip, and you've used vosotros correctly at least once. If more than half the target sounds are consistently wrong, redo the drills and re-shadow the native clip before moving on — this module is the foundation every later checkpoint builds on. If it's distinción specifically that's off, you can proceed, but keep practising it — it's the hardest Peninsular sound to catch by ear in your own speech.

Module 2 — Being & Describing

A1.1 → A1.2 · Weeks 4–6

You'll learn to choose correctly between ser and estar, apply gender and number agreement when describing people and things, and introduce yourself and someone else aloud, using vosotros where it fits.

Watch:

  • 42 Beginners: Understanding SER and ESTAR — LightSpeed Spanish.
  • 47 Easily Learn Spanish: YOU ARE / VOSOTROS ESTÁIS — LightSpeed Spanish.
  • Absolute Beginners Spanish 11-15 — LightSpeed Spanish, gender/number agreement and self-introductions.

Practice: A recurring warm-up builds one ser sentence and one estar sentence each session, rotating topics (nationality, mood, profession, location) so nothing repeats. In week 5 you'll record a 60–90 second monologue introducing yourself and someone else; week 6 combines that with Module 1's pronunciation work in one take.

Checkpoint (20 min). Record a 60–90 second description of yourself and one other person, including a vosotros-directed line, then transcribe it and mark every ser/ estar choice and agreement. Good enough to continue: correct most of the time, at most 1–2 explainable slips, one correct vosotros use. If it's not there yet, redo the relevant drills and re-record on a new topic before Module 3.

Module 3 — Present-Tense Action

A1.2 → A1.3 · Weeks 7–9

You'll conjugate regular -ar/-er/-ir verbs plus the high-frequency irregulars tener, ir, querer, poder, hacer, form questions correctly with qué/dónde/cuándo/cómo/ quién, and ask and answer real personal questions aloud without a script.

Watch and listen:

  • Absolute Beginners Spanish 16-20 — LightSpeed Spanish, regular and irregular present-tense conjugation.
  • Beginners Spanish Podcast 16: Spanish Question Words — LightSpeed Spanish (audio) — question words and word order. This one is audio rather than video; no equally strong video on this exact sub-topic was found, but the module's other two resources are video.
  • 58 Beginners Spanish: ¿Qué vs Cuál? — LightSpeed Spanish.

Practice: The recurring drill rotates through all eight target verbs and all five question words across the module's nine sessions. In week 8 you'll record yourself asking and then answering eight personal questions unscripted; week 9's synthesis chains Modules 1–3 together into one continuous recording — greeting, description, and Q&A.

Checkpoint (25 min). Eight unscripted question-and-answer exchanges, transcribed and checked for word order and conjugation. Good enough to continue: at most two conjugation slips across all eight, and only brief hesitation. If it's not there yet, redo drills for whichever irregular verbs failed and re-run with a fresh set of questions.

Cumulative review — Modules 1–3 (25 min, end of week 9). Closed-notes: recite the alphabet and count to 30, write five ser/estar sentences, conjugate the five irregular verbs in first and second-person plural from memory, and say one vosotros sentence aloud. Any part that fails points back to that module's checkpoint — fix it there before Module 4, since everything after this builds on it being solid.

Module 4 — Daily Life & Routine

A1.3 → A2.1 · Weeks 10–12

You'll use reflexive verbs to describe a routine, tell time and state days and months, express likes and dislikes with gustar-type verbs, and narrate a full minute of your actual day.

Watch:

  • 14 Tell Time in Spanish — LightSpeed Spanish.
  • 44 Beginners Verbs like Gustar — LightSpeed Spanish.
  • Spanish Lesson Early Inter 6: Reflexive Verbs — LightSpeed Spanish.
  • 30 Beginners: Test your listening skills — LightSpeed Spanish, a listening review before Module 5.

Practice: The warm-up rotates reflexive verbs (levantarse, ducharse, vestirse, acostarse, despertarse) alongside stating the time and a gustar opinion. Twice during the module you'll narrate what you actually did that day, in Spanish, using at least three reflexive verbs; the week-12 synthesis layers that on top of Modules 2–3's grammar in one continuous take.

Checkpoint (20 min). Record a 60-second narration of a typical day. Good enough to continue: reflexives correctly formed most of the time, two or more time expressions, gustar/gustan matched correctly at least once. If not, revisit the reflexive and gustar objectives and redo with a different day before Module 5.

Module 5 — Getting Around

A2.1 · Weeks 13–15

You'll use prepositions of place and imperatives to give and follow directions, read simple transport signage, and handle a directions role-play at a natural Peninsular pace.

Watch:

  • Spanish Lesson 35: Abs Beginner Spanish Prepositions — LightSpeed Spanish, prepositions of place.
  • Superbeginner Dreaming Spanish — comprehensible-input listening; filter to Spain-accent hosts.
  • Mi Vida Loca — Full Story — BBC's drama; watch from Episode 3 (about 10 minutes in) for the street/subway directions segment this module actually needs — you don't need the full 22-episode compilation here.

Practice: The warm-up has you invent a fresh four-step route from memory using imperatives and at least two prepositions each time. In week 14 you'll read a piece of transport signage aloud and improvise a directions exchange, then shadow a short native clip about directions three times before reproducing three phrases from memory. Week 15's synthesis plays both a lost traveller and a local giving directions in one recording.

Checkpoint (25 min). Two parts: a recorded directions role-play (both roles), and a shadow-and-compare listening pass on a native clip with comprehension questions answered before checking the transcript, plus reading a real sign or timetable. Good enough to continue: imperatives correct for at least 3 of the directions given, and 3–4 of 5 comprehension/reading questions right before checking. If the listening part specifically isn't there yet, slow the clip down, re-shadow, and retry before moving to Module 6 — don't carry an unresolved listening gap forward.

Module 6 — Eating, Shopping & Lodging

A2.1 → A2.2 · Weeks 16–18

You'll read a menu, shop sign or hotel notice, use comparatives (más/menos... que) and quantity expressions, and role-play ordering food and checking into lodging with polite request forms.

Watch, listen and read:

  • How to Order Food in Spanish — LightSpeed Spanish (audio) — restaurant ordering and polite requests.
  • Beginner Dreaming Spanish — shopping/food comprehensible input, Spain-accent filterable.
  • Make Comparisons in Spanish — SpanishDict grammar guide (article), más/menos... que and tan... como.
  • Unequal Comparisons (part 1): Más/menos ___ que — Señor Jordan. Note: this instructor teaches general/neutral Spanish rather than Peninsular-accented — used here because the comparative grammar itself doesn't vary by dialect, while your Peninsular listening exposure for this module comes from the Dreaming Spanish entry above.

Practice: The warm-up drills two comparative sentences and one polite request form per session, rotating patterns. In week 17 you'll read a menu or notice aloud and then record yourself ordering or checking in; the week-18 synthesis strings together Modules 1, 4, 5 and 6 into a full simulated travel day.

Checkpoint (25 min). A recorded role-play ordering food or checking into lodging, plus reading a real menu or notice and answering questions about it. Good enough to continue: at least one correct polite request, one correct comparative, and 2–3 of 3 reading questions right. If not, revisit polite requests and comparatives and redo with a different scenario.

Cumulative review — Modules 1–6 (25 min, end of week 18). Narrate your routine, give directions, order a meal with a polite request, and — importantly — recite the Module 1 distinción word list once more against the same native clip, to check pronunciation hasn't quietly drifted. Any part failing sends you back to that checkpoint; pay particular attention to the pronunciation recheck, since it's the skill most likely to slide without you noticing.

Module 7 — Talking About the Past

A2.2 · Weeks 19–21

You'll conjugate the regular pretérito and the common irregulars (ir, ser, hacer, tener, estar), learn when to use it versus the present tense, and narrate a past trip or weekend in four to six connected sentences.

Watch and listen:

  • Gordon's Diaries: Help with the Spanish Past 2, Preterite — LightSpeed Spanish.
  • Complete Spanish, Track 58 — Language Transfer (audio), "the dot in the past" for -ar verbs.

Practice: The warm-up rotates one regular verb from each conjugation class plus the five key irregulars, and converts present-tense sentences into pretérito on the spot. Twice in the module you'll narrate something you actually did recently; week 21's synthesis links your destination, activities, purchases and current feelings about the trip into one recording, moving correctly between past events and present-tense reflection.

Checkpoint (20 min). Write out your intended conjugations first, then narrate a past account aloud without reading them, and check what you actually said against your own key. Good enough to continue: at most 1–2 verbs wrong, pretérito correctly chosen over present tense throughout most of the narrative. If not, redo the irregular-verb drills and re-narrate a different weekend before Module 8, since Module 8 teaches the near-future by contrasting it with the past.

Module 8 — Plans, Requests & Unexpected Situations

A2.2 → A2.3 · Weeks 22–23

You'll form near-future statements with ir a + infinitive, handle a simple service or emergency-adjacent exchange (reporting a problem, asking for help) at natural pace, and make and respond to an invitation.

This is the lightest-resourced module in the path — a free, Peninsular-specific video for the emergency/service-request topic specifically proved hard to find, so it leans more on the speaking practice below than the other modules do. Treat the practice tasks here as carrying more of the weight.

Listen and watch:

  • Complete Spanish, Track 7 — Language Transfer (audio), "Voy a: Moving Toward Action," the near-future construction.
  • Spanish Language for Travelers — Rick Steves' Europe. Taught in English, and covers Spain and Latin America together rather than Peninsular-only — the weakest regional match in this path, but still useful for the practical request phrases it teaches.

Practice: The warm-up rotates ir a + infinitive plans across persons, including vosotros at least twice. In week 22 you'll record a 60–90 second improvised exchange reporting a problem or asking for help; week 23's synthesis chains an invitation, a question about it, a reference to something you did before, and a confirmed time and place.

Checkpoint (25 min). Three recordings: three near-future plans, a mock service/ emergency monologue, and a shadow-and-compare listening pass with two comprehension questions. Good enough to continue: ir a + infinitive correct in at least 2 of 3 plans, and — the part that matters most for actual travel — your core request or problem is understandable on playback even with grammar slips. If the mock scenario isn't clear yet, redo it focused purely on getting the message across, then add grammar back on a second take.

Module 9 — Conversation Consolidation

A2.3 → B1-threshold · Weeks 24–26

This is where everything comes together: sustaining a 3–5 minute spoken conversation covering introductions, your daily routine, and at least two travel scenarios, mixing present and pretérito correctly; understanding slow-to-natural Peninsular speech across scenarios without a transcript; and catching and correcting your own errors in real time.

Watch and listen:

  • Mi Vida Loca — full episode playlist — BBC, cumulative review across transport, restaurant and shop scenarios.
  • Intermediate Dreaming Spanish — natural-pace listening without scaffolding, Spain-accent filterable.
  • Complete Spanish, Track 90 (End) — Language Transfer (audio), reviewing the grammar built across the whole course.

Practice: The warm-up has you speak freely for 60 seconds, mark your own errors on playback, and re-record fixing them — a deliberate self-correction loop. Twice you'll listen to an unfamiliar clip and summarise it aloud without pausing or reading anything. The centrepiece is a solo 3–5 minute "conversation" playing both sides of an exchange, covering an introduction, your routine, and at least two travel scenarios, with a short rehearsal run first. An optional cold-questions stretch closes out the module.

Checkpoint (30 min). Record the full self-interview, transcribe it, and mark ser/ estar, agreement and verb-ending accuracy — a self-correction you catch and fix in the moment counts as a partial success, not a failure. Separately, do one listening pass on an unused clip. Good enough to continue: the conversation is sustained close to three minutes covering the required topics, and the listening pass gets the gist.

Cumulative review — Modules 1–9 (25 min). One last full-path retrieval pass: alphabet, counting to 100, three distinción words re-checked against the Module 1 clip, one regular and one irregular verb conjugated in both present and pretérito, an ir a + infinitive sentence, and 15 vocabulary items recalled from memory. This is the last checkpoint before the final check — treat any failure here as worth an extra session before attempting it.

Checkpoints and Progress

Every module ends with a short, self-scored checkpoint built around producing spoken language, not recognising the right answer — because your goal is spoken, a checkpoint you could pass just by recognising correct Spanish wouldn't tell you anything useful. Three techniques are used throughout:

  • Record-and-transcribe — record the task, write out exactly what you said (not what you meant to say), and mark it against a checklist.
  • Shadow-and-compare — repeat a native clip phrase by phrase, then play your recording and the original back to back and mark each target sound.
  • Pre-commit answer key — jot down the grammar you intend to use before recording, then check what you actually said against that, not against memory.

Each checkpoint uses three bands: not yet, good enough to continue, and solid. In Modules 1–4 especially, "good enough to continue" is the expected, on-track result — not a shortfall. "Solid" is a bonus.

The final check (45 minutes, week 26) ties directly back to your original five goals: a 3–5 minute spoken conversation, listening comprehension on two unused clips, a grammar tally from your own conversation transcript, reading comprehension on three real texts, and a two-part vocabulary self-audit (a spot-check of any vocabulary log you've kept, plus a five-minute free-recall list — free recall alone will undercount your real vocabulary, so treat it as a floor, and your logged vocabulary as the better estimate). Passing "good enough to continue" or better on all five parts means the path has met its goal — you're ready to use this Spanish on your trip. If one part falls short, go back to the modules that feed that specific outcome and redo their checkpoints before re-attempting only that part. If a gap is still there after one redo, that's a real limit of what 130 hours can build, not a personal failing.

Three things this plan honestly cannot check for you, no matter how carefully you self-score:

  1. Pronunciation accuracy — specifically distinción and the rolled rr. If you're mispronouncing them, you typically can't hear it yourself; that's what makes them hard. Shadowing catches gross errors but not subtle drift.
  2. Real-time, unpredictable listening — every listening check here uses a fixed clip with a knowable answer. Tracking an actual person who interrupts or rephrases unpredictably is a different, harder skill.
  3. Genuine conversational turn-taking — playing both sides of a conversation yourself removes the central difficulty of real conversation: not knowing what the other person will say next.

All three point to the same fix: real conversation with a native or fluent speaker, ideally around Modules 1, 3 and 8–9. See Time and Cost for what that would cost.

What your EUR 50 would actually be best spent on. Since nothing in this plan costs money, the real question is what the unspent budget should go toward. Based on the three things self-assessment genuinely cannot verify — pronunciation, real-time listening, and turn-taking — the highest-value use of that money is one or a handful of paid conversation-practice sessions (a platform like italki or Preply, or any language-exchange option you have access to), timed around Modules 1, 3, and 8–9. As a rough, unverified sense of scale, conversation- tutoring sessions on these platforms commonly run somewhere in the EUR 8–20 range for 30–45 minutes — treat that as an illustrative starting point to research, not a quote.

Other costs to know about, even though none are priced in this plan:

  • A DELE certification exam fee is not part of this plan's scope — the goal here was deliberately retargeted away from certified B1 toward practical competence. If you later decide you want the certificate, that's a separate undertaking with its own costs.
  • A vocabulary-logging tool — a notebook or spreadsheet is free and sufficient, but worth setting up from week 1 rather than week 25.
  • Recording equipment — a phone microphone is assumed and is almost always adequate; worth confirming yours works before Module 1's first checkpoint.

What Comes Next

Completing this path gets you to a strong A2 / B1-threshold level: able to hold a real conversation, understand slower native speech in common travel situations, and read everyday signs and menus, with Peninsular pronunciation and vosotros built in from the start. Some things were deliberately left out to make the honest 130-hour budget work, and you should know what they are before you assume you've covered everything a "B1" label might imply elsewhere:

  • The imperfect tense (for extended past narrative and habitual description)
  • The subjunctive mood (for wishes, doubt, and formal requests)
  • Compound/perfect tenses (haber + participle)
  • Sustained written composition and formal register
  • Vocabulary beyond the ~1,000-word travel/social core this plan targets
  • Latin American dialectal forms — this plan is Peninsular-only throughout, by design

None of these are needed for the goal you actually confirmed; adding them back in would have meant cutting the speaking practice you asked for. If you decide afterward that you want certified B1 or beyond, these are exactly the gaps to fill next, and you'll be starting that from a genuinely useful base rather than from zero.

After the trip, the most natural next steps are: continuing with more advanced Dreaming Spanish content at natural pace, picking up the imperfect and subjunctive through a structured course, and — if the conversation practice recommended above went well — simply continuing it. Real use, especially during the trip itself, will do more for your Spanish than any additional module would.

One module is worth flagging again here: Module 8 (plans, requests, and unexpected situations) has the thinnest resource coverage in this path — a free, Peninsular-specific video for reporting problems or asking for help in a service context was genuinely hard to find.
