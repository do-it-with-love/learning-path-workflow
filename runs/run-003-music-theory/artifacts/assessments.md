# Assessments — Music Theory for Score Reading and Analysis

## Summary

Every one of the 10 modules gets a self-markable checkpoint (15–30 minutes) built on the
one genuine advantage this subject has over most self-study material: a piano answer key.
Wherever a check asks the learner to notate or label something, it also asks them to play
it and listen — "does this chord/scale/cadence sound like what I said it was" is a real,
independent check that a language or math learner rarely gets. Where the ABRSM grade
framework the curriculum is aligned to applies, checks are phrased as small worked drills
the learner marks against material they generate themselves (a circle-of-fifths reference
sheet, a triad-quality recipe card), not against an answer the workflow would have to
invent. The stated weakness — slow, roughly-equal-speed reading in both clefs — is made
visible with an objective bars-correctly-read-per-minute timing, taken as a baseline in
Module 1 and re-taken at each of the three cumulative reviews and the final check, so
progress (or its absence) is a number, not a feeling. Cumulative reviews land after
Modules 3, 6 and 9, each a short retrieval sweep across everything so far plus a fluency
re-check. The final check is the Module 10 capstone rubric itself, applied to the written
analysis of Clementi's Sonatina in C, Op. 36 No. 1, 1st movement, and is written to cover
all five target outcomes in `requirements.md` directly. Total self-assessment time across
the course is approximately 5.5–6 hours, comfortably inside the 8-hour slack the curriculum
already reserved — checkpoints are overhead on top of study time, not a redirection of it.

## Findings

### Module 1: Key Signatures, the Circle of Fifths & Reading Fluency

**Check (30 min).**
1. Write out 10 key signatures at random (mix of sharp and flat sides, 0–4 accidentals).
   Name the major key for each, timing yourself. Self-check against a circle-of-fifths
   diagram you draw from memory as part of the same session.
2. From memory, write out the sharp order (F C G D A E B) and the flat order (the same
   list reversed). Check against the diagram you just drew.
3. **Fluency baseline (objective, keep this number).** Pick an unseen 8–12 bar excerpt
   with both treble and bass clef from your own materials. Read the note names aloud at a
   steady pace (a metronome or phone timer helps), timing yourself. Compute **bars
   correctly read per minute** — check correctness by playing what you named and
   confirming the notes match the score. Write this number down; you will repeat this
   exact exercise three more times.

**Rubric.**
- *Not yet* — fewer than 7/10 signatures named correctly, or each one requires counting
  sharps/flats from scratch; the sharp/flat order can't be recited without checking a
  reference.
- *Good enough to continue* — 8–9/10 signatures correct within a few seconds each; the
  sharp/flat order recited with at most one slip; fluency baseline recorded with an error
  rate (wrong note names) under 15%.
- *Solid* — 10/10 instantly on sight, both sides of the circle; sharp/flat order recited
  without hesitation; fluency baseline recorded with an error rate under 5%.

**Go/no-go.** If key-signature naming lands in *not yet*, redrill with the circle-of-fifths
diagram in view for 10 minutes, then redo part 1 with a fresh set of 10 signatures before
starting Module 2 — later modules assume this is automatic, not calculated. The fluency
number itself is never a blocker at this stage; it exists to be compared against later, not
judged now.

### Module 2: Intervals — Number and Quality

**Check (25 min).**
1. Notate 12 intervals on staff paper (mix of treble and bass clef, sizes 2nd–8ve,
   qualities major/minor/perfect and at least two diminished or augmented). For each, name
   the number and quality, then play both notes on the piano and count semitones to
   confirm.
2. Given 8 stated notes, spell an interval above or below each as instructed (e.g. "a
   minor 6th below D4"). Play each pair to confirm by ear and by semitone count.
3. Play a major scale starting on an unfamiliar tonic (not C, G, F, or D) using only the
   W-W-H-W-W-W-H pattern from memory, no key-signature lookup. Check it against the key
   signature you'd expect for that key from Module 1.

**Rubric.**
- *Not yet* — under 60% of intervals correctly named or spelled; the major-scale pattern
  can't be reproduced without counting on the keyboard for every step.
- *Good enough to continue* — 75–85% correct on naming and spelling; occasional hesitation
  on compound intervals or diminished/augmented qualities; the scale pattern reproduces
  correctly with one or two self-corrections caught by ear.
- *Solid* — over 90% correct including the tritone and compound intervals; the scale
  pattern is applied instantly to any tonic and matches the expected key signature without
  needing to check it afterward.

**Go/no-go.** If *not yet*, restrict practice to simple intervals within an octave (defer
compound, diminished and augmented) using keyboard semitone-counting as the primary method,
then redo the 12-interval quiz with a fresh set before Module 3 — minor-scale construction
there is built directly on reliable interval spelling.

### Module 3: Minor Scales & Relative/Parallel Keys

**Check (25 min).**
1. Pick 3 different tonics. For each, notate the natural, harmonic and melodic minor
   scale. Play each one and listen: does the harmonic minor's raised 7th sound tense/
   exotic, does the melodic minor's ascending form sound smooth and descending form
   revert to natural? Correct any note that sounds wrong before checking it against the
   interval-pattern method from Module 2.
2. Given 6 major key signatures, state the relative minor for each from memory, self-check
   against the circle-of-fifths reference from Module 1.
3. Take a short (4–8 bar) unseen excerpt whose key signature is ambiguous between a major
   key and its relative minor. Determine which by playing the final chord/cadence and
   listening for the leading tone.

**Rubric.**
- *Not yet* — scales contain wrong notes even after playing and listening; more than 2/6
  relative minors misidentified; cannot use the final-chord evidence to resolve the
  major/minor ambiguity without being told the method.
- *Good enough to continue* — scales are correct after one self-correction pass at the
  piano; 5/6 relative minors correct; resolves the ambiguity using the cadence evidence
  with a reminder of what to listen for.
- *Solid* — scales correct on the first written attempt, including the raised notes in
  harmonic/melodic forms; 6/6 relative minors instant; independently reaches for cadence
  evidence to disambiguate without prompting.

**Go/no-go.** If *not yet*, rebuild natural minor only first (defer harmonic/melodic
refinement), specifically re-listening for the difference the raised 7th makes, then retry
with one new tonic before Module 4 — triad construction there assumes scale degrees are
reliable.

### Cumulative review — after Module 3 (25 min)

A short retrieval sweep, not new material: 5 key signatures (Module 1), 4 intervals mixed
number/quality (Module 2), 2 minor scales spelled and played (Module 3), plus a **repeat of
the fluency timing exercise** from Module 1 (same method, a new unseen excerpt of similar
length) — record bars/minute and error rate next to the Module 1 baseline.

**Go/no-go.** If exactly one of the three retrieval categories is weak, revisit only that
module's material for one short session before continuing — do not re-do all three. If two
or more categories are weak, that is a forgetting pattern, not a fluke: spend a full extra
20–30 minute session across the weak modules before starting Module 4. The fluency number
is not pass/fail here; if it has not moved from baseline, note it and continue — three more
checkpoints remain to establish a real trend.

### Module 4: Triad Construction & Quality

**Check (25 min).**
1. Build 8 triads from given root+quality instructions (mix of major, minor, diminished,
   augmented) by writing them on staff. Play each and listen for the character (major
   bright/stable, minor darker, diminished tense/unstable, augmented ambiguous) before
   confirming with the interval recipe (major = 4+3 semitones, minor = 3+4, diminished =
   3+3, augmented = 4+4).
2. Without playing first, identify the quality of 6 pre-notated triads by sight. Then play
   each to confirm.
3. Take one triad, write and play its two inversions, and identify each by the visual gap
   pattern on the staff (root position: stacked thirds; first inversion: 4th then 3rd;
   second inversion: 3rd then 4th).

**Rubric.**
- *Not yet* — fewer than 5/8 triads built correctly; sight quality identification needs
  interval-counting for every chord; inversions can't be told apart visually.
- *Good enough to continue* — 6–7/8 built correctly with one piano self-correction each;
  4/6 sight identifications correct; inversions identified with a brief pause to check the
  gap pattern.
- *Solid* — 8/8 correct on first write; 6/6 sight identifications instant, no playing
  needed to confirm; inversions identified immediately.

**Go/no-go.** If *not yet*, re-drill the four interval recipes explicitly, restrict the
retry to root-position triads only, and redo the 8-triad build with a fresh set before
Module 5 — primary-triad spelling there assumes fluent triad building, not derivation from
scratch each time.

### Module 5: Primary Triads in a Key

**Check (20 min).**
1. Pick 4 major keys. Spell the I, IV, V and vi triads in each from memory (no writing out
   the scale first), then play and check against the quality rules from Module 4.
2. State which scale degrees give major triads and which give minor triads in a major key
   (I, IV, V major; ii, iii, vi minor; vii° diminished), from memory.
3. Take one page of a simple piece from your own materials. Find and circle 5 places where
   a I, IV or V triad appears (block or broken). Verify each by playing the notes together
   and comparing the sound to the triad quality expected at that scale degree.

**Rubric.**
- *Not yet* — fewer than 2/4 keys spelled correctly from memory; the major/minor degree
  pattern needs to be looked up; 0–1 of 5 triads correctly located in the score.
- *Good enough to continue* — 3/4 keys correct; the degree pattern stated with a brief
  pause; 3/5 triads located and confirmed by ear.
- *Solid* — 4/4 instant; degree pattern stated without hesitation; 5/5 triads located
  confidently, including ones written as broken chords or with the bass note doubled.

**Go/no-go.** If *not yet*, re-derive I, IV, V, vi in one key only using the Module 4
method, memorise that one key's shapes before adding a second, and retry before Module 6 —
Roman-numeral labelling there needs primary triads as raw material, not something derived
mid-exercise.

### Module 6: Roman-Numeral & Lead-Sheet Analysis

**Check (25 min).**
1. Take (or write) an 8-chord diatonic progression. Label every chord with a Roman numeral
   including correct case for quality (upper case major, lower case minor, ° for
   diminished). Independently convert the same progression to lead-sheet letter names.
   Play through both labellings and confirm they describe the same notes.
2. Take the opening 4–8 bars of the capstone piece (Clementi, Sonatina in C, Op. 36 No. 1,
   1st movement). Attempt a first-pass Roman-numeral labelling of its chords. Check each
   label by playing the chord and comparing to what that scale degree should sound like.

**Rubric.**
- *Not yet* — fewer than 5/8 progression chords correctly labelled; case errors on quality
  are frequent; the lead-sheet conversion doesn't match the Roman-numeral labels when
  played.
- *Good enough to continue* — 6–7/8 correct after a self-correction pass at the piano;
  case rule applied correctly; the Clementi first pass correctly identifies the clearly
  tonic and dominant chords even if a couple of passing or less-obvious chords are left
  uncertain.
- *Solid* — 8/8 correct on first attempt; instant lead-sheet conversion; the Clementi
  first pass labels every chord in the excerpt with only genuinely ambiguous chords (if
  any) flagged as uncertain.

**Go/no-go.** If *not yet*, revisit the case-and-quality mapping against Module 5's
major/minor degree pattern, redo the 8-chord drill with a new progression, and retry before
Module 7 — cadence identification depends on reliable Roman-numeral labels, not on
recalculating them mid-exercise.

### Module 7: Cadences

**Check (20 min).**
1. Write 6 two-chord progressions using Roman numerals from Module 6 (include at least one
   each of V–I, IV–I, ending on V, and V–vi). Play each and name the cadence type (perfect,
   plagal, imperfect/half, interrupted) *by ear first*, then confirm against the numerals.
2. Recall the ambiguous major/relative-minor excerpt from Module 3. Locate its final
   cadence and use it to confirm the key identification made then.
3. Locate one cadence in the opening phrase of the capstone Clementi excerpt, name its
   type, and verify by playing and listening for whether it resolves (perfect) or feels
   open (imperfect).

**Rubric.**
- *Not yet* — fewer than 3/6 cadence types correctly named; the Module 3 key ambiguity
  isn't resolved even with the cadence in hand; no cadence can be located in the Clementi
  excerpt without help.
- *Good enough to continue* — 4–5/6 correct; resolves the Module 3 ambiguity with a
  reminder of what to listen for; finds a plausible cadence point in the Clementi excerpt,
  even if its exact classification needs double-checking against the numerals.
- *Solid* — 6/6 correct by ear alone before checking the numerals; independently resolves
  the Module 3 ambiguity; correctly classifies the Clementi cadence unprompted.

**Go/no-go.** If *not yet*, re-drill the four cadence "sound signatures" by ear (play each
type three times, describe in one phrase how it feels — resolved, half-open, plagal-soft,
surprising), then retry with 4 fresh progressions. Module 8 (seventh chords) does not
depend on this and can proceed in parallel, but cadence fluency must be solid before
Module 9, where phrase boundaries are found by ear via cadences.

### Module 8: Seventh Chords & Extended Analysis

**Check (20 min).**
1. Spell a V7 chord (root, 3rd, 5th, 7th) in 4 major keys, including the key of the
   capstone piece. Play each and listen for the tritone dissonance between the 3rd and
   7th that gives V7 its "wants to resolve" pull.
2. Scan a short passage (4–8 bars, containing at least one dominant chord before a
   cadence) for a four-note chord. Identify whether it is V7, label it with the Roman
   numeral, and verify by playing and listening for the resolution to I.

**Rubric.**
- *Not yet* — fewer than 2/4 V7 chords spelled correctly; the characteristic dissonant
  sound isn't recognised even after playing it; the passage scan finds no V7 without heavy
  hints.
- *Good enough to continue* — 3/4 correct; the tritone sound is recognised once played and
  described; the passage scan locates the V7 with one self-correction.
- *Solid* — 4/4 instant, including the capstone piece's key; the tritone sound is
  recognised by ear before checking the notes; the V7 is located and labelled confidently
  in an unfamiliar passage.

**Go/no-go.** If *not yet*, redo the V7-spelling drill specifically in the capstone
piece's key (that is the one that matters for Module 10), re-listen for the tritone several
times. Module 9 does not depend on this and can proceed, but this must be revisited before
Module 10 capstone work begins, since the capstone requires labelling any dominant sevenths
present.

### Module 9: Phrase Structure & Musical Form

**Check (25 min).**
1. Take a short simple piece (8–16 bars, ideally similar in style to the capstone). Play
   through and mark where each phrase ends using cadence evidence from Module 7 (listen for
   the "breath" or resting points). Count the phrases.
2. Determine whether the piece is binary (AB) or ternary (ABA) by comparing the opening
   material to what follows it, both by ear and by comparing the notated material directly.
3. Write 2–3 sentences describing how the sections relate — e.g. does a new section move to
   a different key area, does material return exactly or in varied form, does a cadence
   type mark each boundary.

**Rubric.**
- *Not yet* — phrase boundaries can't be located without being told where to listen;
  binary/ternary is guessed without pointing to evidence; the written description is vague
  ("they sound different") with no reference to cadence, key, or returning material.
- *Good enough to continue* — most phrase boundaries correctly located using cadence
  evidence; binary vs. ternary correctly identified after a brief self-check against the
  notated material; the description references at least one concrete harmonic or thematic
  feature.
- *Solid* — all phrase boundaries located confidently and quickly; form identified
  immediately and justified with both harmonic (key area) and thematic (returning
  material) evidence; the description is specific and accurate about how sections relate.

**Go/no-go.** If *not yet*, revisit Module 7's cadence-spotting first, since form
identification depends on it, then redo phrase-marking on a shorter 8-bar example. Retry
before Module 10 — accurate form identification is one of the three legs the capstone
analysis needs.

### Final check

The final check is the rubric applied to the Module 10 capstone document, and it is written
to cover the five target outcomes from `requirements.md` directly.

| Target outcome | Where it is assessed |
|---|---|
| 1. Read/name notes and rhythms in both clefs, measurably faster than baseline | Final (4th) fluency timing, same method as Module 1, compared against the Module 1 baseline number |
| 2. Identify a piece's key, including relative major/minor | Capstone document's key section |
| 3. Label chords via triads/Roman-numeral/lead-sheet, including simple sevenths | Capstone document's chord section |
| 4. Identify formal structure and describe section relationships | Capstone document's form section |
| 5. Produce a written capstone analysis combining key, chords and form | The capstone document as a whole |

**Fluency check (10 min).** Repeat the timed sight-reading exercise from Module 1 a fourth
time, same method, an unseen excerpt of similar length and difficulty. Compare bars/minute
and error rate against all three prior recordings. *Good enough to continue*: a clear
upward trend across the four data points, with the final error rate under 10%. *Solid*: at
least a doubling of bars/minute from the Module 1 baseline at an error rate under 5%. If the
trend is flat or reversed, this is diagnostic, not a capstone blocker on its own — it means
sight-reading practice needs to become a deliberate weekly habit going forward, separate
from theory study.

**Capstone analysis rubric** — what a good analysis of key, chords and form actually
contains, in observable terms:

- *Not yet* — the key is stated without citing both the signature and a cadence, or is
  wrong; more than roughly a quarter of the chords are unlabelled or turn out wrong when
  played and checked; the form section is a vague claim ("it has two parts") with no
  cadence or thematic evidence behind it; the three sections read as separate,
  disconnected notes rather than one analysis.
- *Good enough to continue* — the key is correctly identified and both the signature and a
  cadence are cited as evidence; at least 80–85% of chords are correctly labelled and
  verified by playing them (an occasional passing or non-primary chord left approximate is
  acceptable); the form is correctly classified as binary or ternary, phrase boundaries are
  mostly correctly marked from cadence evidence, and at least one sentence explicitly
  connects a harmonic feature to the form (e.g. "the B section ends with a half cadence,
  which is why it feels unresolved until A returns"); the document reads as a single
  connected analysis, not three separate lists.
- *Solid* — the key is identified instantly and confidently from both signature and
  cadence; every chord, including any dominant sevenths, is labelled correctly and
  consistently in one notation style throughout; every phrase boundary is correctly marked
  and the form is justified with both harmonic (key area) and thematic (returning material)
  evidence; the synthesis draws a specific causal link between the harmonic plan and the
  formal structure, not just a list of both.

**Baseline honesty.** Someone twelve weeks into a first theory course, starting from note
names and the major scale only, should expect to land in *good enough to continue* on the
capstone — that is the target the curriculum was designed to hit, not a fallback. *Solid*
corresponds to roughly ABRSM Grade 5 fluency and is a stretch goal well beyond what 48
hours of study can be expected to produce; landing there on every leg is a bonus, not the
bar for having met the goal.

**Go/no-go.** If any one of the three legs (key, chords, form) is *not yet*, the capstone is
not complete — do not treat a partially correct document as done. Revisit precisely the
weak leg: key errors → redo the Module 1 (signature) and Module 3/7 (relative-minor and
cadence evidence) checks on a fresh short excerpt; chord-labelling errors → redo the Module
4–6 drills (and Module 8 if the errors are specifically on sevenths) on a shorter excerpt
first, then re-attempt the full piece; form errors → redo the Module 9 phrase-marking
drill. Then redo only the weak section of the capstone document, not the whole thing —
expect one extra 30–45 minute session, not a restart. If all three legs land at *good
enough to continue* or better, the stated goal in `requirements.md` has been met.

## Open Questions

- **Genuinely ambiguous chord analysis.** Some passing or non-chord tones in real
  repertoire admit more than one defensible Roman-numeral reading. A self-check by ear
  confirms the labelled notes are present and roughly right-sounding, but cannot adjudicate
  between two theoretically valid analyses the way a teacher's judgement could. Where the
  capstone hits such a passage, the honest self-assessment is "plausible and internally
  consistent," not "provably correct" — a teacher, a theory forum, or a published analysis
  of the same piece would be needed to fully resolve it.
- **Ear-based verification has a ceiling.** Every check in this artifact leans on "play it
  and listen" as an answer key, which is a genuine strength of this subject but assumes
  the learner's ear can catch a wrong note. A learner with underdeveloped relative pitch
  might notate an error and not hear that it's wrong. The checks partially guard against
  this by cross-referencing notated pitches against the interval/quality rules as a second,
  non-aural check, but a listener with trained ears (a teacher or a more advanced player)
  would catch errors this framework cannot.
- **Fluency target in absolute terms.** This artifact tracks bars-per-minute as a trend
  against the learner's own Module 1 baseline rather than an absolute target (e.g. "60
  bars/minute"), because no baseline number exists yet at design time. If an absolute
  target matters to the learner, an external benchmark (an ABRSM sight-reading grade
  standard, or a teacher's assessment) would be needed to convert the trend into a graded
  standard.
