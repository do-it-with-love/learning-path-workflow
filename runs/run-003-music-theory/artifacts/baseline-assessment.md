# Baseline Assessment — Music Theory for Score Reading and Analysis

## Summary

Placed on the ABRSM (Associated Board of the Royal Schools of Music) graded music
theory scale, the learner sits at the pre-Grade-1 / early-Grade-1 boundary: they have
the absolute rudiments (note names, the major scale) but none of the harmonic vocabulary
(intervals, triads, Roman-numeral/functional analysis) that Grade 1–3 theory assumes, and
their clef-reading fluency — while present in both clefs — is slower than the sight-reading
rate implicit in that same grade band. Mode is **full**: this is a beginner in formal
theory but not an absolute beginner, so a self-administered placement check is included
below to confirm or correct this reading before curriculum design proceeds.

## Findings

### Level scale

The learner's goal (read a score in both clefs; analyse key, chords, form) maps cleanly
onto the internationally recognised **ABRSM graded music theory syllabus** (Grades 1–8,
UK exam board, Ofqual-accredited, in use since 1933) and its standard topic dependency
chain, which is the closest thing music theory has to a CEFR-style ladder for a learner
at this stage:

| ABRSM-aligned stage | Core content | Typical prerequisite for |
|---|---|---|
| Rudiments (pre-Grade 1) | Note names, staff/clef reading, note values, simple time signatures, the major scale | everything below |
| Grade 1 | Key signatures (major, up to a few sharps/flats), simple/compound time, intervals (number + quality, simple cases) | triad construction |
| Grade 2–3 | Triad construction (major/minor/diminished/augmented) on any degree, primary triads in a key, minor scales (natural/harmonic/melodic), simple transposition | Roman-numeral analysis |
| Grade 4–5 | Roman-numeral / functional harmony (I, IV, V, vi, ii, primary and secondary triads), cadences (perfect, imperfect, plagal, interrupted), simple seventh chords (V7), figured bass basics | phrase/period-level analysis |
| Grade 5–6+ | Phrase and period structure, binary/ternary form, modulation to closely related keys, more developed harmonic analysis of short pieces | full-piece analysis (the learner's capstone) |

This chain — rudiments → intervals → triads/key-based harmony → Roman-numeral/functional
analysis → cadences → phrase/form — is also the standard progression used in general
university-level tonal harmony pedagogy, not just the ABRSM syllabus specifically; ABRSM
is used here as the named, citable version of it because it is the field's most widely
recognised graded framework and gives the learner an external benchmark they can look up
independently.

### Prerequisite gaps

These are absent (or unconfirmed) skills that a module 1 built on "read a score and
analyse key/chords/form" would otherwise silently assume the learner already has:

1. **Key signature recognition** — naming a key from its signature on the staff is not
   the same skill as spelling the major scale from a given tonic. Not yet confirmed
   present; module 1 must teach or verify this explicitly rather than assume it.
2. **Interval identification (number and quality)** — no stated exposure at all. This is
   a hard prerequisite for triad construction and must precede it, not run alongside it.
3. **Minor scale forms** — only the major scale was claimed; the goal requires
   distinguishing relative major/minor by ear/eye (target outcome 2), which needs at
   least natural and harmonic minor.
4. **Triad quality and inversion recognition on the page** — chord construction is
   explicitly absent; this is the single largest gap relative to the goal (target
   outcomes 3 and 5 both depend on it directly).
5. **Functional/Roman-numeral vocabulary** — explicitly absent; cannot be assumed even
   at a "basic I–IV–V" level.
6. **Cadence identification** — absent, and depends on both triads and functional
   labelling being solid first; needed for the key-confirmation part of target outcome 2.
7. **Fluent two-clef sight-reading at tempo** — literacy exists in both clefs but at a
   "slow" rate in both; module 1 cannot assume the learner can read fast enough to
   analyse a piece while playing it without a dedicated fluency-building strand running
   alongside the harmony content.

No gap was found in note-name literacy or major-scale spelling; these can be assumed
solid from module 1 onward without re-teaching, per the learner's own claim.

### Placement check

Self-administer this before module 1. It has 8 questions, ordered from rudiments to
early functional analysis, deliberately spanning one level either side of the assessed
placement to catch a mis-estimate in either direction.

1. Name the notes on the bass-clef staff lines (bottom to top): G, B, D, F, A. On a
   sheet of manuscript paper (or by naming aloud), write or say the note on the *middle*
   bass-clef line without counting up from the bottom. *(Rudiments check.)*
2. Which major key has the signature of two sharps? Which has one flat?
   *(Key signature recognition — currently unconfirmed.)*
3. Name the interval between C and G. Is it perfect, major, or minor?
   *(Interval quality — assessed absent.)*
4. Spell the notes of a D major triad in root position.
   *(Triad construction — assessed absent.)*
5. Spell the notes of a D minor triad in root position, and say what changes versus
   question 4. *(Triad quality contrast.)*
6. In the key of C major, what is the Roman numeral for a triad built on G? Is it
   upper- or lower-case? *(Roman-numeral analysis — assessed absent.)*
7. A phrase ends V–I. What is this cadence called? What about IV–I?
   *(Cadence naming — assessed absent, depends on Q6.)*
8. Look at any two-line piano excerpt (e.g. the first two bars of a simple hymn or
   minuet). Without stopping to count semitones, name every note in both hands within
   30 seconds. *(Clef-reading fluency under time pressure.)*

**Answer key:**
1. Bottom to top on bass clef: G2, B2, D3, F3, A3 — middle line is D3.
2. Two sharps = D major; one flat = F major.
3. C–G is a perfect fifth.
4. D major triad: D–F♯–A.
5. D minor triad: D–F–A (the third is lowered a semitone versus D major).
6. G in C major is the V chord, upper-case (major triad).
7. V–I is a perfect (authentic) cadence; IV–I is a plagal cadence.
8. No fixed answer — self-score on completion within 30 seconds without hesitation.

**Scoring rule:**
- 0–2 correct (excluding Q1, which should always be correct): baseline is *lower* than
  assessed — flag this in module 1 kickoff so rudiments get more time than currently
  planned.
- 3–5 correct: confirms the assessed placement above (rudiments solid, everything from
  intervals onward genuinely absent) — proceed with the curriculum as scoped from this
  assessment.
- 6–7 correct: baseline is *higher* than assessed, most likely in interval/triad
  recognition — the curriculum can compress the early triad-construction module and
  spend the saved time on functional analysis and form instead.
- Q8 is scored separately and does not move the harmony placement: if the learner cannot
  complete it within 30 seconds without hesitation, the fluency-building strand in the
  schedule should not be shortened even if harmony questions score well.

## Open Questions

- **Key signature recognition** was not directly stated by the learner as known or
  unknown. Knowing the major scale by construction does not guarantee fluent recognition
  of key signatures on a staff, so it was conservatively marked "absent (unconfirmed)"
  rather than assumed. Placement check question 2 resolves this directly.
- **Whether "roughly equally slow" in both clefs means genuinely comparable fluency or
  just that the learner hasn't noticed a difference** could not be resolved from the
  requirements text alone. Placement check question 8 (timed sight-reading of both
  hands) is the concrete way to settle this before the schedule fixes a fluency-building
  pace for each clef.
- **No standard theory framework distinguishes "beginner-intermediate pianist" from
  "beginner theorist" on the same scale** — these are different axes (performance vs.
  analysis). The ABRSM practical grades and theory grades are in fact separate syllabuses
  that don't have to move in lockstep, and this assessment deliberately only places the
  learner on the *theory* scale, leaving their stated piano performance level as
  supporting context rather than a scale point.
