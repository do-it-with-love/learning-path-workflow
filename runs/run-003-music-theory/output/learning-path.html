Music Theory for Score Reading and Analysis — Your Learning Path

Overview

You will go from knowing note names and the major scale to reading a piano score confidently in both clefs and producing a full written analysis of a real piece — key, chords, and form combined into one coherent document. The path takes you through key signatures, intervals, minor scales, triad construction, primary and diatonic harmony, Roman-numeral and lead-sheet analysis, cadences, simple seventh chords, and phrase/form, finishing with a capstone analysis of the first movement of Clementi's Sonatina in C major, Op. 36 No. 1 — a standard, widely used beginner teaching piece. The plan is reading-led, as you asked for, with a short self-marked checkpoint at the end of every module and three larger cumulative reviews along the way.

One thing to know before you commit: this schedule is tight. Total real work comes to about 47.77 hours against your 48-hour budget (4 hours/week × 12 weeks) — roughly 14 minutes of slack across three months. Seven of the eleven active weeks sit right at the weekly ceiling with no cushion of their own. This is workable, but it means you should read “Before You Start” and “Time and Cost” below before you begin, not after you fall behind.

Before You Start

What you need already. A piano or keyboard, and the ability to play while you read — every check in this path uses “play it and listen” as part of the answer key. No purchases are required: every resource is either free to borrow, free to download, or a free web page.

What to set up in the first day or two, before your first reading session:

  1. Open a free Internet Archive / Open Library account. Nine of your thirteen resources are books borrowed through Internet Archive's lending library — genuinely free, but not instant. Borrowing needs an account, popular titles can have a waiting list, and loans typically run for a limited period (commonly 14 days, renewable if no one else is waiting). With only about 14 minutes of slack in the whole schedule, a two-week wait for a book is the single most likely thing to derail this plan — not the theory itself.
  2. Request your first three books now, before you start reading: Wharram's Elementary Rudiments of Music (Module 1), Ottman & Mainous's Rudiments of Music (Module 2), and Benward, Saker & White's Music in Theory and Practice (Module 3). Do this on day one, not when Module 1 begins — if any of them is on hold, you find out with time to act instead of mid-module.
  3. Sort out staff/manuscript paper and a metronome. Several exercises ask you to notate on staff paper (free blank-staff PDFs exist if you don't already have some — printing is the only cost, and it isn't priced here) and to use a metronome (a free phone or web metronome app works fine). Neither is bundled as a “resource,” so set them up before Module 1 rather than discovering the gap mid-drill.

Placement check — do this before Module 1. Your stated level places you at the rudiments / early-Grade-1 boundary on the standard graded framework this path follows: solid on note names and the major scale, but with no interval, triad, or functional vocabulary yet, and clef-reading fluency that's present in both hands but slower than target. Confirm that reading is right for you with this eight-question self-check before you start (10–15 minutes):

  1. Name the notes on the bass-clef staff lines (bottom to top: G, B, D, F, A). Say or write the middle line without counting up from the bottom.
  2. Which major key has a signature of two sharps? Which has one flat?
  3. Name the interval between C and G. Is it perfect, major, or minor?
  4. Spell the notes of a D major triad in root position.
  5. Spell the notes of a D minor triad in root position, and say what changes versus question 4.
  6. In the key of C major, what is the Roman numeral for a triad built on G? Upper- or lower-case?
  7. A phrase ends V–I. What is this cadence called? What about IV–I?
  8. Look at any two-line piano excerpt (e.g. the first two bars of a simple hymn or minuet). Without stopping to count semitones, name every note in both hands within 30 seconds.

Answer key: (1) G2–B2–D3–F3–A3, middle line D3. (2) Two sharps = D major; one flat = F major. (3) Perfect fifth. (4) D–F♯–A. (5) D–F–A (third lowered a semitone). (6) V, upper-case (major triad). (7) V–I is a perfect (authentic) cadence; IV–I is a plagal cadence. (8) No fixed answer — self-score on whether you finished within 30 seconds without hesitating.

Scoring:

  • 0–2 correct (question 1 aside, which should always be right): your starting point is a little earlier than this plan assumes — expect to spend more of Module 1's time on rudiments than scheduled, and don't be surprised if the early weeks feel fuller than planned.
  • 3–5 correct: this confirms the starting point the path is built for. Proceed as scheduled below.
  • 6–7 correct: you're a little ahead, most likely on intervals or triads — you can move a little faster through Modules 4–5 and put the saved time toward Module 6 and the phrase/form work in Module 9.
  • Question 8 is scored separately and doesn't move your placement either way: if you couldn't complete it cleanly in 30 seconds, don't shorten the fluency drills in each module even if the harmony questions went well — they're tracking two different skills.

Modules

Module 1 — Key Signatures, the Circle of Fifths & Reading Fluency (4.10 h)

Covers

naming any major key from its signature on sight, spelling the order of sharps and flats from the circle of fifths without counting, and starting to measure your two-clef reading speed against a real number rather than a feeling.

Read

Wharram, Elementary Rudiments of Music, pp. 20–55 (~2.3 h) and musictheory.net's “Key Signatures” lesson (~20 min).

key-signature flashcards (20 at random, name each in under 3 seconds, then confirm by playing the tonic triad); a timed one-minute bass-clef sight-reading drill — this is your fluency baseline, the first of five measured points across the path; naming keys from four unfamiliar signatures before playing to check; and a first look at the capstone piece's own key signature.

a 30-minute check — name 10 key signatures from memory (aim for 8–9/10 within a few seconds each, ideally 10/10), recite the sharp and flat orders, and log your fluency baseline (aim for under 15% error, ideally under 5%). If key-signature naming isn't solid, spend 10 more minutes with the circle-of-fifths diagram in view and retry before Module 2 — everything from here assumes this is automatic, not calculated.

Module 2 — Intervals: Number and Quality (4.72 h)

Covers

naming the number and quality of any interval on the staff in either clef, spelling an interval above or below a given note, and recognising the step pattern (W-W-H-W-W-W-H) that builds a major scale.

Read

Ottman & Mainous, Rudiments of Music, pp. 60–100 (~2.7 h), plus teoria.com's interactive intervals tutorial (~40 min) for extra self-testing.

interval flashcards you play as well as name (does a major 3rd really sound brighter than a minor one?); a bass-clef fluency drill that adds interval-spotting on top of reading speed; naming six real melodic leaps from a piece you're working on; and labelling every interval in the capstone's opening two bars.

a 25-minute check — 12 notated intervals to name and play (aim for 75%+, ideally 90%+ including compound and altered intervals), 8 intervals to spell from a stated note, and a major scale built on an unfamiliar tonic purely from the step pattern. If it's shaky, restrict yourself to simple intervals within an octave, counted at the keyboard, and retry before Module 3 — minor-scale construction depends directly on reliable interval spelling.

Module 3 — Minor Scales & Relative/Parallel Keys (4.42 h + a 0.42 h cumulative review)

Covers

constructing natural, harmonic and melodic minor scales from any tonic, naming the relative minor for any major key and vice versa, and telling a major key from its relative minor using signature plus cadence evidence — not signature alone.

Read

Benward, Saker & White, Music in Theory and Practice, pp. 40–75 (~2.9 h).

writing and playing all three minor forms on five tonics, listening for where the raised notes change the sound; spotting the “extra” accidental while sight-reading in a minor key; deciding major-vs-relative-minor on three unfamiliar excerpts using the final cadence, not just the signature; and revisiting Module 1's four excerpts to see if any answer changes with this new method.

a 25-minute check on scale spelling, relative-minor recall, and one ambiguous excerpt resolved by ear. If it's not solid, rebuild natural minor only first before adding the harmonic and melodic forms, and retry with one new tonic before Module 4.

Module 4 — Triad Construction & Quality (5.62 h)

Covers

building major, minor, diminished and augmented triads from any root, recognising quality on sight without recalculating intervals, and spotting root position versus the two inversions on the page.

Read

Benward & Saker, Music in Theory and Practice, Volume 1 (2014 edition), pp. 90–130 (~3.3 h).

building and playing all four triad qualities on 12 roots across three sessions, hearing the quality change back to back; naming triad quality while sight-reading a bass-clef passage of block or broken chords; working out root, quality and inversion by ear before checking on paper; and labelling every triad in the capstone's opening four bars.

a 25-minute check — building 8 triads from instructions, identifying 6 by sight, and telling inversions apart by their visual gap pattern. This is the single largest jump from your starting point, and it's sized generously (5.62 h real, against 5 h budgeted) for exactly that reason. If it's not solid, re-drill the four interval recipes explicitly and restrict practice to root-position triads before retrying — Module 5 assumes fluent triad building, not deriving one from scratch each time.

Module 5 — Primary Triads in a Key (4.33 h)

Covers

spelling the I, IV, V and vi triads in any major key from memory, knowing which scale degrees give major versus minor triads, and finding primary triads directly on a real score.

Read

Piston & DeVoto, Harmony, pp. 15–45 (~3.0 h). This is a tight fit against the module's hours — if the reading runs long, stop at the I–IV–V–vi spelling drills and treat scale-degree identification as review rather than pushing the whole module over.

spelling I, IV, V, vi from memory in six keys; naming them aloud while sight-reading a I–IV–V–I progression; locating primary triads “by feel” in a piece you know, then checking against your spelled set; and matching Module 4's capstone triads to this module's I/IV/V/vi labels.

a 20-minute check — spelling primary triads in four keys, stating the major/minor degree pattern from memory, and locating five primary triads in a real score. If it's not solid, re-derive I, IV, V, vi in one key only, memorise that one fully, then add a second before retrying.

Module 6 — Roman-Numeral & Lead-Sheet Analysis (6.12 h + a 0.42 h cumulative review)

Covers

labelling triads with Roman numerals and correct upper/lower case for quality, converting between Roman-numeral and letter-name (lead-sheet) labels, and applying this to a first pass at the capstone piece.

Read

Piston, Principles of Harmonic Analysis, pp. 1–40 (~4.3 h — this is the biggest reading load of the path, split across two weeks), plus Open Music Theory's “Performing a harmonic analysis” web chapter (~30 min), which walks through locating cadences and building a functional reduction.

writing the full diatonic triad set (I through vii°) in three keys, then playing the major-major-minor-major-major-minor-diminished pattern by ear; naming Roman numerals with correct case while sight-reading; labelling an unfamiliar passage both ways (Roman numeral and lead-sheet) and justifying any ambiguous call; and a first Roman-numeral pass on the capstone's opening phrase — this exact pass gets reused directly in the capstone module, so it's worth doing carefully.

a 25-minute check — labelling an 8-chord progression both ways, then a first-pass attempt at the Clementi opening. If it's not solid, revisit the case-and-quality mapping against Module 5's degree pattern and retry with a fresh progression before Module 7.

Module 7 — Cadences (2.58 h)

Covers

identifying perfect, plagal, imperfect and interrupted cadences from a two-chord progression, using a cadence to confirm or correct a key call, and finding a cadence in the capstone piece.

Read

Piston, Harmony (1948 edition), pp. 40–55 (~1.5 h) — a different Open Library edition from the one used in Module 5; if you can only get hold of one physical or borrowed copy of Piston's Harmony, either edition covers both the Module 5 and Module 7 material, since the two are substantively the same text.

naming eight cadence types by ear before checking against the numerals; sight-reading phrase endings and naming the cadence type as fast as you read it; returning to Module 3's ambiguous excerpts and using the cadence to settle them; and finding and naming the clearest cadence in the capstone's opening phrase.

a 20-minute check on naming six cadences by ear, resolving the earlier key ambiguity, and locating the Clementi cadence. If it's not solid, re-drill the four cadence “sound signatures” by ear and retry with fresh progressions — cadence fluency needs to be solid before Module 9, where phrase boundaries are found by ear via cadences.

Module 8 — Seventh Chords & Extended Analysis (4.43 h)

Covers

spelling a dominant seventh (V7) chord in any major key, recognising and labelling V7 in a score, and extending a Roman-numeral analysis to include it.

Read

Kostka & Payne, Tonal Harmony, pp. 200–230 (~3.0 h) — a widely used undergraduate harmony text, used here just for its dominant-seventh chapter.

spelling and resolving V7 in six keys, listening for the tritone pull toward I; spotting V7 while sight-reading; finding and labelling a V7 in an unfamiliar passage and checking whether it resolves as expected; and upgrading any V chord in the capstone's opening-phrase analysis to V7 where it should be.

a 20-minute check — spelling V7 in four keys (including the capstone's own key) and scanning a passage for one. If it's not solid, redo the spelling drill specifically in the capstone's key, since that's the one that matters for Module 10 — Module 9 doesn't depend on this and can proceed in parallel, but this must be settled before the capstone.

A candid note: if your weeks are running long and something has to give, this is the module the plan itself identifies as most droppable — your target outcome only requires simple seventh chords, and V7-spotting could be folded into Module 6 at a recognition-only level instead of its own module. Skipping it restores a genuine ~9-hour buffer across the rest of the course. It isn't necessary to drop it — the schedule fits without doing so — but it's the honest answer to “what do I cut if I need room.”

Module 9 — Phrase Structure & Musical Form (5.12 h + a 0.50 h cumulative review)

Covers

finding phrase boundaries from cadences, telling binary (AB) from ternary (ABA) form, and describing how two sections of a piece relate to each other.

Read

Green, Form in Tonal Music, pp. 1–40 (~3.3 h) — a dedicated form-analysis text, used here only for its opening chapters on phrase and binary/ternary form.

marking phrase boundaries by ear in three short pieces, then checking against genuine cadence points; a full-piece fluency check timed against your Module 1 baseline; deciding binary or ternary on two unfamiliar pieces with your reasoning written down; and mapping the capstone's opening section — phrase boundaries, cadence types, and whether the two halves are harmonically similar or contrasting. This mapping feeds directly into Module 10.

a 25-minute check on phrase-marking, form identification, and describing a section relationship in writing. If it's not solid, revisit Module 7's cadence-spotting first (form identification depends on it), then retry on a shorter example.

Module 10 — Capstone: Full Analysis of Clementi's Sonatina in C major, Op. 36 No. 1 (5.00 h)

Covers

bringing everything together into one written analysis of the capstone piece — its key (with evidence), every chord (Roman numeral or lead-sheet), and its formal structure, combined into a connected document rather than three separate lists.

Score

the IMSLP page for Clementi's 6 Piano Sonatinas, Op. 36 has several free public-domain scans plus a clean modern typeset edition by Chris Sawer for the Mutopia Project (2003) — use the Mutopia edition for legibility unless you prefer working from a period facsimile. Sonatina No. 1 is confirmed to be in C major. (The exact bar count of the movement wasn't independently re-verified beyond the key — if anything in the score turns out to need a concept outside Modules 1–9, treat it as an edge case to note in your written analysis rather than a sign you've missed something.)

a final fluency check first (sight-read the whole piece once, log your fifth and final measures-per-minute figure); extending your Module 6 Roman-numeral pass to the entire movement, flagging any chord you're genuinely unsure of rather than guessing silently; completing the phrase/form map for the whole piece using Module 9's method; and then writing one connected 1–2 page document — key with evidence, the chord progression in prose using your Roman-numeral labels, and the form and how the sections relate. Read it aloud once: every claim should be pointable-to on the score. If a sentence isn't, it's a guess — revise or flag it. If you have energy left, an optional 15-minute stretch exercise asks you to add a short second paragraph analysing one section from a different angle than your main document did.

Score your finished document against three legs:

  • Key: correctly identified, citing both the signature and a cadence as evidence.
  • Chords: at least 80–85% correctly labelled and verified by playing them (an occasional approximate passing chord is fine).
  • Form: correctly classified as binary or ternary, with phrase boundaries mostly right and at least one sentence explicitly connecting a harmonic feature to the form (e.g. “the B section ends with a half cadence, which is why it feels unresolved until A returns”).

If all three legs reach this bar, you've met the goal. Landing there — not a flawless, Grade-5-level analysis on every leg — is genuinely the target this whole path was designed to hit, starting from note names and the major scale twelve weeks earlier.

Checkpoints and Progress

Every module check leans on the one real advantage this subject has over most self-study material: a piano answer key. Wherever a check asks you to notate or label something, it also asks you to play it and listen — “does this actually sound like what I said it was” is a genuine, independent check most subjects don't offer.

Rubric levels. Each module check scores you as not yet, good enough to continue, or solid. “Good enough to continue” is the real bar for moving on — you don't need solid anywhere along the way, including on the capstone itself. Each module section above gives you the specific go/no-go rule and what to redo if you land in not yet; the consistent principle is: fix the weak piece with a short, targeted retry, not a restart of the whole module.

Good enough to continue

Solid

The third of the three levels a module check can score you at.

Your fluency trend. The slow, roughly-equal two-clef reading speed you started with is tracked as one concrete number — measures correctly read per minute — timed and logged five times across the path: a baseline in Module 1, then again in the cumulative reviews after Modules 3, 6, and 9, and a fifth and final time in the capstone module. Watching that number move (or not) across five data points is one of the more motivating parts of this plan — it turns “am I actually getting faster?” into something you can look at rather than guess at.

A caveat worth carrying with you. Every check here relies on your own ear to catch a wrong note, and a beginner ear has a real ceiling — an underdeveloped sense of relative pitch can let an error through undetected. The written cross-checks (e.g. Module 6 catching a Module 4 slip) guard against this partially, but not completely. Likewise, a few passages in real repertoire admit more than one defensible Roman-numeral reading; where the capstone hits one, “plausible and internally consistent” is the honest self-assessed standard, not “provably the only correct answer.” A teacher or a more experienced player would catch things this framework can't — worth knowing, not a reason not to proceed on your own.

Money: £0 of your £30 budget. All thirteen resources are free — nine Internet Archive loans, three web pages, one public-domain score. Nothing here needs to be purchased, so a “free-only variant” isn't a different, cut-down version of this path — it is this path, as designed.

A paid alternative — buying secondhand copies of the Kostka, Piston, or Benward texts, which are commonly available used — would cost money but remove the lending-queue and 14-day-loan risk entirely, let you read offline, and let you annotate the book directly. Given how thin this schedule's time margin is, that resilience is worth considering if a book you need turns out to be on a long hold queue.

Time: 47.77 hours of your 48-hour budget — about 14 minutes of slack. This is real, bottom-up arithmetic (reading + practice + checkpoint time per module, not a rough estimate), and it is tight: seven of the eleven active weeks sit at or within a minute of the weekly ceiling with no cushion of their own. In practice this means:

  • Any single session that runs long has almost nowhere to absorb it except Week 12's buffer (which itself has only about 4 hours and 22 minutes free, after a small carryover item from Week 11).
  • If you fall behind and need to recover real breathing room rather than just hoping the buffer covers it, dropping Module 8 (seventh chords) is the one change that restores a genuine margin — about 9 extra hours, roughly 19% of the whole course — because your stated goal only requires recognising simple seventh chords, which could be folded into Module 6's checkpoint instead of run as its own module. This isn't necessary; the plan fits as designed. It's the honest answer if something has to give.

Hidden costs — not priced above, but real:

  • An Internet Archive account is required to borrow any of the nine books, and isn't mentioned anywhere except here.
  • Lending queues. Popular titles — Kostka & Payne's Tonal Harmony (Module 8) and Piston's Harmony (used in Modules 5 and 7) especially — can have a hold queue for a single scanned copy.   • A metronome: a free phone or web app is enough, but a physical one would be an unbudgeted spend if you'd rather have one.
  • Piano/keyboard access is assumed already in place, since you're already a beginner-intermediate pianist — flagged only because it's the largest cost class if that assumption doesn't hold.

What Comes Next

Finishing the capstone gets you to roughly the Grade 4–5 boundary on a standard graded theory ladder: solid diatonic harmony, Roman-numeral and lead-sheet analysis, cadences, simple dominant sevenths, and phrase/binary/ternary form on a real piece. That's a real, usable foundation for reading and understanding most simple-to-intermediate piano repertoire — it is not the end of the subject.

Deliberately left out of this path, because a 48-hour beginner course to a single piece's analysis has no room for them, but worth knowing about as your natural next steps:

  • Modulation to closely related or distant keys, and pivot-chord analysis.
  • Four-part chorale-style voice leading and part-writing rules.
  • Figured bass realisation.
  • Secondary dominants and other chromatic or altered chords beyond a plain V7.
  • Full sonata-form analysis (exposition/development/recapitulation) — the capstone uses only a short, simple movement, not a full development section.
  • Species counterpoint.
  • Formal aural training or dictation, beyond the informal ear-checks built into every exercise here.
  • Transposition practice beyond recognising a key signature.
  • Repertoire beyond solo piano.

If you want to keep going afterward, the next natural step on the same graded ladder is the material just past what Module 10 reaches — modulation, secondary dominants, and fuller part-writing — and the two harmony texts you'll already have spent time with in this path (Piston's Harmony and Kostka & Payne's Tonal Harmony) both continue directly into exactly that material in their later chapters, so you won't need to start from a new book to take the next step.
